1.1 Turning Lead into Gold

The idea of turning lead into gold has fascinated alchemists and scientists for centuries. While it is not feasible to do this through traditional alchemical methods, modern nuclear physics has made it possible to transmute elements. This process involves bombarding lead with high-energy particles, causing it to lose protons and neutrons, ultimately transforming it into gold.

The process is not practical for large-scale production of gold due to the immense energy required and the low yield of gold produced. However, it serves as a fascinating example of the principles of nuclear physics and the potential for manipulating matter at the atomic level.

Let’s say we have a lead pellet (Pb‑208) that is 2 cm thick with a diameter of 4 cm. We want to bombard it with protons to transmute it into gold (Au‑197). The proton beam has a current of \(100\,\mu\text{A}\) and an energy of 1 GeV. Let’s also assume that the entire beam is absorbed by the lead pellet. For reference, the lead pellet has a density of \(\rho_{Pb} = 11.34\,\text{g cm}^{-3}\) and a molar mass of \(M_{Pb} = 207.2\,\text{g mol}^{-1}\).

The reaction we are interested in is: p + 20882Pb → 19779Au + 4p + 8n This can be written in a more compact form as: \[ ^{208}\!\text{Pb}(p,4p8n)^{197}\!\text{Au}. \] This means that when a proton collides with a lead nucleus, it ejects 4 protons and 8 neutrons, resulting in the formation of gold. Notice the 1:1 ratio of gold atoms produced per reactant proton.

The desired mass of gold we want to produce is: \[ m_{\!Au}=0.1\;\text{ozt}=0.1\times31.1035\;\text{g}=3.11035\;\text{g}. \] Converting this mass to a number of atoms gives \[ N_{\!Au}=\frac{m_{\!Au}}{M_{Au}}N_{A}=\frac{m_{\!Au}}{197\;\text{g mol}^{-1}}\,N_{A}, \] where \(M_{Au}\) is the molar mass of gold and \(N_{A}\) is Avogadro’s constant.

A 1 GeV proton beam with current \(I=100\,\mu\text{A}\) delivers a proton flux of \[ \dot N_{p}=\frac{I}{e}=\frac{100\times10^{-6}\;\text{C s}^{-1}}{1.602\times10^{-19}\;\text{C}}=6.24\times10^{14}\;\text{p s}^{-1}, \] where \(e\) is the elementary charge.

Assuming the beam is fully stopped in the 2 cm thick pellet, the areal density of target nuclei is \[ n_{t}=\frac{\rho_{Pb}\,t}{M_{Pb}}N_{A}, \] with \(\rho_{Pb}\) the density of lead, \(t\) the target thickness, and \(M_{Pb}\) the molar mass of lead.

The production rate of \(^\text{197}\!\text{Au}\) through the direct reaction \(^{208}\!\text{Pb}(p,4p8n)^{197}\!\text{Au}\) is then \[ \dot N_{Au}=\dot N_{p}\,n_{t}\,\sigma_{(p,4p8n)}, \] where \(\sigma_{(p,4p8n)}\) is the reaction cross‑section at 1 GeV.

Finally, the irradiation time required to accumulate the desired number of gold atoms is \[ t_{\text{irr}}=\frac{N_{Au}}{\dot N_{Au}}=\frac{m_{\!Au}\,M_{Pb}}{M_{Au}\,\rho_{Pb}\,t\,\sigma_{(p,4p8n)}}\,\frac{1}{\dot N_{p}}\,N_{A}. \] This expression links the beam parameters, target properties, reaction cross‑section, and the desired mass of gold.
